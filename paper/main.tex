\begin{abstract}
In 2026 Major League Baseball began letting teams challenge ball--strike calls against an automated ball--strike
(ABS) system, and a team keeps every challenge that succeeds. How much do teams leave on the table by how they
challenge? The published zone rule, applied to the public pitch locations, reproduces \GeomRepro\% of the \GeomN{}
reviewed rulings, so the call the system would make can be reconstructed for unchallenged pitches too, and the
challenge decision becomes a binary-response model fitted on challenged and unchallenged opportunities alike. The model
identifies the total decision noise, not how much of it, or of the players' lapses, a new decision rule could remove.
We define an accounting index, the wins per team-season by which a dynamic-programming benchmark learned from the data
exceeds the teams' challenges when only the decision rule changes, estimate it out of fold (the benchmark is learned on other games and scored on held-out games), and treat the unidentified shares as
sensitivity parameters. Under a model with player effects and lapses the index is \Gap{} wins per team-season, with a
bootstrap interval of \GapCI{} that refits the whole procedure and is conditional on the model; it is \GapLamOne{} if the
lapses could be removed, \GapRhoHalf{} if half the noise variance could, and \PCFLo--\PCFHi{} across the models that fit
the decisions at least as well as a homogeneous one. Most of the gap is targeting: the rule ``challenge if
$p\ge\RuleDelta/(g+\RuleDelta)$'', with $p$ the probability that the call is wrong and $g$ the win-probability stake,
recovers \GapRuleGapPct\% of it within the model at the teams' own challenge rate.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

From the 2026 season, Major League Baseball (MLB) calls balls and strikes with human umpires but lets the batter,
catcher or pitcher appeal a call to the automated ball--strike (ABS) system, a Hawk-Eye measurement of the pitch
against a rule-defined zone. Each team starts a game with two challenges; a challenge that overturns the call is
kept and one that fails is lost. Over the regular season teams made \NChallenges{} challenges, about two per
team-game, of which \NOverturned{} succeeded. Retention changes the economics: a challenge can be reused as often as
it is right, so its value grows with the opportunities left and the right confidence threshold moves with the inning,
the score and the inventory. MLB's Baseball Savant site prices a challenge at a fixed 0.2 runs
\citep{Savant2026abs,Tango2026costbenefit}; practitioners have proposed state-dependent heuristics
\citep{Hamm2026cls,Goldstein2026crv} and approximate dynamic programs \citep{Arora2026use,Namuduri2026runs}; retained
review rights were first modelled in tennis \citep{ClarkeNorman2012,NadimpalliHasenbein2013,AbramitzkyEtAl2012}.

\emph{The statistical problem.} Valuing a challenge policy needs to know how well the challenger can tell that a call
was wrong, and the data are decisions selected on exactly that judgement. Three difficulties follow, and this paper is
organised around them. First, \emph{identification of the response is not identification of the information}: even
with the truth known for every pitch, the challenge data identify the total noise between the truth and the
decision, not how much of it is perception that no rule can remove and how much is a criterion that wobbles from
pitch to pitch, or lapses of attention, that a disciplined rule could remove. What a team could gain therefore depends
on an intervention the data cannot pin down. Second, \emph{the benchmark is learned}: the stakes come from a
win-probability engine fitted to the same season, and the decision model, the players' beliefs and the dynamic program
are fitted to it too. A benchmark that selects pitches on estimated quantities and is scored on the same estimates is
scored optimistically, as an estimated optimal policy is \citep{KitagawaTetenov2018,AtheyWager2021}. Third, \emph{the
decision model is uncertain}: the models that fit the decisions differ in what they imply, and the best-fitting one
is the most expensive to refit.

\emph{Contributions.} (i) A known truth: on the public 2026 coordinates the published zone rule reproduces the
reviewed rulings to \GeomRepro\%, so the ABS label is reconstructed for unchallenged pitches as well, and the decision
model is fitted on all eligible opportunities rather than on the selected challenges: the known truth removes the
selective-labels problem that usually prevents evaluating human decisions against a rule
\citep{LakkarajuEtAl2017,KleinbergEtAl2018} (Section~\ref{sec:model}). (ii) An accounting index
defined by an intervention: the primary intervention changes only the decision rule; the perceptual share $\rho$ of the
decision noise and the removable share $\ell$ of the lapses are not identified and are reported as a sensitivity grid.
The index is an accounting quantity within a pooled decision model, not a causal effect and not a bound
(Section~\ref{sec:interv}). (iii) An out-of-fold estimator: a full policy cross-fit in which the engine, the decision model,
the beliefs, the dynamic program and the rules are all learned on other games and applied unchanged to held-out games,
whose flips and observed challenges are valued by an engine fitted to those games alone, in the spirit of
cross-fitting in double machine learning \citep{ChernozhukovEtAl2018}; its target is the value of the benchmark a team
could learn from data, not of an oracle, and its out-of-fold scoring removes the optimism of selecting on estimated stakes, as for
estimated treatment rules \citep{HiranoPorter2009,AndrewsEtAl2024} (Section~\ref{sec:honest}). (iv) Inference for the whole learned procedure under
the best-fitting model: a bootstrap that refits everything, the decision model's hyperparameters included, in every
replicate, and a coverage study on the real game stream (Section~\ref{sec:eval}). Substantively, most of the gap is in
which pitches teams challenge, not how often (Section~\ref{sec:mech}). Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the chain from
what is observed to the policy value, and which link carries which assumption.

\section{The ABS challenge system and the data}\label{sec:data}

\noindent{\itshape Rules.}\hspace{1em}After a called ball or strike the batter, catcher or pitcher may challenge
immediately; the call is compared with the ABS zone, a rectangle whose width is the plate and whose top and bottom are
fixed fractions of the batter's height, evaluated at the plate's midpoint. A successful challenge flips the call and
the team keeps the challenge; a failed one is lost. A team that has used up its challenges receives one in each extra
inning \citep{MLB2025abs}. No challenge in the feeds is made with none left (Section~\Sref{sec:counters} of the
Supplementary Material; \citealp{JanousekPesta2026supp}).

\paragraph{Data} We use the \NGamesABS{} ABS games of the 2026 regular season (\NGames{} completed, \NGamesNoABS{}
at temporary venues without the system dropped). Pitch tracking comes from Statcast; the challenge events come from the
MLB Stats API live feeds, whose counts reconcile with the league's totals and the feeds' own counters. A called pitch
is joined to its Statcast record on its pre-pitch count and call type within the plate appearance: the count only
rises, so the key is unique. Joining on the pitch number instead attaches the wrong
location whenever one source counts an automatic ball or strike as a pitch and the other does not, which a check of the
call description cannot detect when both pitches are balls or both strikes; \NRenumbered{} called pitches are
numbered differently by the two sources, and on all of them the count-joined Statcast location agrees with the feed's own to at most \RenumMaxDiff\,in. Only \NUnmatched{} called pitches find no count-consistent Statcast pitch; with those
whose Statcast record has no coordinates, \NNoLoc{} called pitches are unlocated, and all \NChallenges{} challenges are
located. Of these, \NChBatter{} were made by batters on called
strikes (\OverturnBatter\% overturned), \NChCatcher{} by catchers on called balls (\OverturnCatcher\%) and
\NChPitcher{} by pitchers (\OverturnPitcher\%). Pitches thrown by position players (\NPosPitch{}) are not challenge
opportunities \citep{Savant2026abs}. We pool pitchers with catchers as the fielding side.

\paragraph{Stakes} A count-level win-probability (WP) engine estimated from 2026 pitch transitions gives, for every
called pitch, the stake $g$: the WP the challenging team gains if the call is flipped. Each count cell of the
plate-appearance transition table is shrunk toward the table pooled over counts. In \NNonMono{} called pitches this
empirical engine makes a ball worth less than a strike to the batting team; a flip there is worth zero. The engine is
league-average; Section~\ref{sec:stakes} validates its contrasts.

\section{A known truth and a decision model}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{The ABS truth}\label{sec:truth}
Let $d$ be the ball's radius plus the signed distance of the ball's centre to the ABS rectangle, in inches, positive
when the ball touches the zone (the strike region is the rectangle grown by the radius, with rounded corners),
computed from the Statcast location, and let $e$ be the nearest edge (bottom, top, inside, outside). Write $w=\pm d$
with the sign chosen so that $w>0$ means the umpire's call was wrong under the rule, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:truth}
W = \mathbf 1\{w>0\}, \qquad Y = \begin{cases} W & \text{with probability } 1-\pi,\\ 1-W & \text{with probability } \pi, \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $Y$ is the ABS ruling on a challenged pitch and $\pi$ a small reconstruction-disagreement rate (the five mismatches of Section~\Sref{sec:mismatch} are boundary ties within 0.01\,in of an edge and one coordinate
disagreement between sources), not a
measured error rate of the ABS system. From 2026 Statcast reports the
location at the plate's midpoint \citep{SavantCSV2026}; taken as published, these coordinates reproduce \GeomRepro\%
of the \GeomN{} rulings under the rule (\GeomMismatch{} mismatches, all within 0.01\,in of an edge or where Statcast and
the live feed disagree on the location), square corners reproduce \GeomReproSquare\% (on the \GeomNOld{} rulings of a description-based join; Section~\Sref{sec:geometry}), and shifting either zone line
does not improve the fit. A smooth truth model with region offsets and Gaussian measurement noise is not preferred to the deterministic one, and BIC prefers~\eqref{eq:truth} by \BICSmoothMinusDet; the fitted mislabel rate is
$\pi=\ContamPct\%$. The agreement is measured on reviewed pitches, which cluster near the edges; on all called pitches
the label from the live feed's own coordinates differs from the Statcast label for \LabelDifferPct\%, mostly within a
quarter of an inch of an edge. Both sources share the Hawk-Eye measurement, so this checks the plane and the rounding,
not the error against the ABS system's own measurement (Section~\Sref{sec:geometry}). Statistical models of the
human-called zone go back at least to \citet{ZimmermanEtAl2019}; here the zone is the rule's, and what we estimate is
the challenger's perception of it.

\subsection{The decision model and its likelihood}
The player who may challenge (the batter on a called strike, the catcher on a called ball) observes a signal
$s=w+\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon\sim N(0,\sigma_{r}^2)$, and challenges iff $s>\kappa(x)$, where $x$ collects the
challenges left, the inning, the count and $\log g$. In the homogeneous specification used as comparator below, $\kappa$ is piecewise linear in
$\log g$ with hinges at fixed values ($-5$, $-4$, $-3.2$, about the 40th, 80th and 95th percentiles), and both $\kappa$
and $\sigma_r$ shift by zone region (Table~\Sref{tab:params} gives every term). For a called pitch at distance $w$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pch}
\begin{aligned}
\Prob(\text{challenge}\mid w)&=\Phi\!\left(\frac{w-\kappa}{\sigma_r}\right),\\
\Prob(\text{challenge}, Y=y\mid w)&=\Phi\!\left(\frac{w-\kappa}{\sigma_r}\right)\bigl[(1-\pi)\mathbf 1\{y=W\}+\pi\,\mathbf 1\{y\ne W\}\bigr].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The likelihood uses the challenge opportunities (a challenge held, the side that may challenge) within 8\,in of the
edge, \NLikBatter{} for batters and \NLikCatcher{} for the fielding side; the window trims the fit only, and the
policies see every opportunity. This is where the known truth does its work: $w$ is observed for every called pitch, so
the challenge curve is traced out over wrong calls that were not challenged as well as over those that were. A lapse
rate $\lambda$, $\Prob(\text{challenge}\mid w)=(1-\lambda)\Phi\{(w-\kappa)/\sigma_r\}$ \citep{WichmannHill2001},
captures the curve's levelling off below one far from the zone.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:ident}
Within a side and region, with $\kappa$ fixed by the covariates: (a) the challenge curve~\eqref{eq:pch} identifies
$(\kappa,\sigma_r)$ as soon as $w$ takes two distinct values; (b) the log-likelihood is additive in $\pi$ and in
$(\kappa,\sigma_r)$; (c) if the signed margin ABS measures equals the tracked margin plus independent $N(0,c^2)$ noise, the
fitted $\sigma_r$ is $(\sigma_r^2+c^2)^{1/2}$ (an additive error in the scalar margin; Gaussian error in the two
coordinates is not additive in the margin near a corner); (d) if the player sees $s_p=w+\sigma_p\varepsilon_1$ and challenges iff
$s_p+\sigma_\nu\varepsilon_2>\kappa(x)$, with independent standard normal $\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2$, the challenge
curve depends on $(\sigma_p,\sigma_\nu)$ only through $\sigma_r^2=\sigma_p^2+\sigma_\nu^2$.
\end{proposition}
\noindent Part (a) is the identification of a probit with an observed regressor (Jacobian determinant of absolute value
$\phi_1\phi_2|w_1-w_2|/\sigma_r^3$); with covariates it is a full-design condition, and at the fitted values the
Jacobian of the challenge curve on a grid of distances has full column rank for both sides. Parts (a)--(d) are verified in Mathematica
(Section~\Sref{sec:kernel}). By (c), $0.3$\,in of tracking noise would bias the homogeneous model's $\sigma_r$ (the comparator of
Section~\ref{sec:estimates}) up by \DetCoordBiasBatter\% for
batters and \DetCoordBiasCatcher\% for catchers. Part (d) is the crux of this paper: the data identify the decision
noise $\sigma_r$, not its perceptual part $\sigma_p$, and the same holds for the lapse rate, whose removable share is
not identified. A lapse rate $\lambda$ is identified only through the parametric shape: the response is still rising
where the data end (\TailFourBatter{} batter and \TailFourCatcher{} catcher opportunities wrong by more than 4\,in in the
season), so $\lambda$ is read from the curvature of the probit over $w\in(0,4]$, not from an observed plateau
\citep{Prins2012}. Simulating from a fitted homogeneous baseline model (Section~\Sref{sec:perception}) and refitting from a deliberately wrong start, the bias in $\sigma_r$ over the \RecovConv{} of \RecovN{} replicates whose refit converged is \RecovBiasBatter\% for batters and \RecovBiasCatcher\% for catchers
(Section~\Sref{sec:perception}).

\subsection{Estimates and the set of models}\label{sec:estimates}
Table~\ref{tab:variants} lists the decision models. The \emph{primary model} is player random effects on $(\kappa,\log\sigma_r)$, integrated with $11\times11$ Gauss--Hermite
nodes, and a lapse rate. It is better than the homogeneous model by \PDBIC{} BIC points. Its decision noise at the

\section{The intervention, the benchmark and the estimand}\label{sec:interv}

\paragraph{The intervention} A recommendation changes something, and what it can recover depends on what. The
\emph{primary intervention} changes only the decision rule: the player sees what they see now and attends as they
attend now. By Proposition~\ref{prop:ident}(d) the fitted noise may be partly criterion noise $\sigma_\nu$ that a
disciplined rule removes; let $\rho=\sigma_p^2/\sigma_r^2$ be its perceptual share. In the lapse models let $\ell$ be
the share of lapses a change of rule removes. The primary intervention is $\rho=1$, $\ell=0$: the benchmark player's
signal is as noisy as the decisions reveal and every lapse stays. Secondary interventions take $\rho<1$ (criterion
noise removed) or $\ell>0$ (lapses removed). The data cannot decide $(\rho,\ell)$, so Section~\ref{sec:pid} reports the
loss on a grid, $\rho\in\{1,0.75,0.5,0.25\}$ and $\ell\in\{0,0.5,1\}$; it does not reach the perfect-perception end
$\rho\to0$, where the gap is far larger. Within a model the benchmark's value is smallest at the primary intervention:
the behavioural signal $s_p+\sigma_\nu\varepsilon_2$ is a garbling of $s_p$, so by Blackwell's theorem
\citep{Blackwell1953} the less noisy signal is worth at least as much to the same decision problem (checked in
Mathematica on a grid of one-opportunity problems), and fewer lapses lose fewer opportunities. Blackwell's ordering is a statement about Bayes-optimal decisions; for the estimated,
pooled-model benchmark it is a regularity we check numerically (Table~\ref{tab:pid}), and it orders benchmark values
within the model, not what a team loses in real games.

\paragraph{The decision problem} Index the remaining opportunities of a team by $t$ and the challenges held by $c$. At
each opportunity a stake $g\ge0$ and the player's calibrated probability $p$ that the ruling will overturn the call
arrive. A challenge gains $g$ and keeps the challenge with probability $p$, and loses it otherwise. With
$\Delta_t(c)=V_t(c)-V_t(c-1)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bellman}
V_t(c) = V_{t-1}(c) + \E_t\bigl[(pg-(1-p)\Delta_{t-1}(c))^+\bigr],\qquad
\text{challenge iff}\quad p \ge \frac{\Delta_{t-1}(c)}{g+\Delta_{t-1}(c)} .
\end{equation}
The 0.2-run rule replaces $\Delta$ by a constant 0.2 runs and $g$ by the run value of the flip; the ``one-line rule''
replaces $\Delta$ by a constant $\delta$ in WP.
\begin{assumption}\label{ass:exo}
The pair $(p,g)$ at opportunity $t$ has law $F_t$, independent of the past and of $c$.
\end{assumption}
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:mono}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:exo}, for all $t$ and $c\ge1$: $\Delta_t(c)\ge\Delta_t(c+1)\ge0$ and
$\Delta_t(c)\ge\Delta_{t-1}(c)$, so the optimal threshold falls as the game runs down and rises as challenges run out.
For the rule ``challenge iff $p\ge\delta/(g+\delta)$'', with $c_t$ the challenges it holds,
$D=\Delta_{t-1}(c)$ and $I_t(c,g)$ the interval between $D/(g+D)$ and $\delta/(g+\delta)$,
\[ V^\star_T(c_0)-V^{\delta}_T(c_0)=\sum_t\sum_{c\ge1}\Prob^\delta(c_t=c)\,\E_g\!\int_{I_t(c,g)}|p(g+D)-D|\,dF_t(p\mid g). \]
\end{theorem}
\noindent The DP we use (below) has the score in its state and a random number of opportunities, so it does not satisfy
Assumption~\ref{ass:exo}; the monotonicity in time (the marginal value of a challenge falling as the opportunities run out) fails at
\DPRiseEntPct\% of its computed entries and the concavity in inventory at \DPConcEntPct\% (Section~\Sref{sec:proofs}), and the threshold movements we report are computed, not deduced from the theorem. The
second part is the performance-difference identity \citep{KakadeLangford2002}: the rule errs exactly on
$I_t$ and pays the Q-gap there. With a bounded density of $p$ the integrand is second order in $\delta-D$, which is why
the value curve can be flat; the calibrated posterior here is a step function, so that bound is only heuristic for the
application, and the near-optimality of one constant below is an empirical finding (proofs are in
Section~\Sref{sec:proofs}, with stylised results on the square-root growth of a retained challenge and a flat-optimum
bound that the calibrated game does not satisfy).

\paragraph{The benchmark} The calibrated $p$ is the posterior $\Prob(Y=1\mid s,\text{cell})$ implied by the decision
model, estimated by isotonic regression \citep{BarlowEtAl1972} of simulated truth on the signal within cells of side,
zone region, count class and stake tercile, on independent simulated draws. The DP is solved per team side; its state
is the half-inning, the home score differential at the start of the half-inning (clipped at $\pm6$), the opportunity
index and the challenges held. The number of a team's opportunities in a half-inning is random with the empirical law
at that half-inning and score, every half-inning counting, including the \NHalvesZero{} with no opportunity for either
side; the stakes and beliefs of the opportunities are drawn from the pool of that half-inning and score; extra innings
are an exact stationary block with the top-up rule. Outs, bases and count enter only through the stake, and a
successful challenge changes neither the count process nor the runs that follow, so the DP is optimal for a pooled
opportunity model, not for baseball. We call its policy the \emph{pooled-model benchmark}. The loss against it is not
a bound in either direction on the loss against the best policy for the real game process.

\paragraph{The estimand} For a policy $a$ let $V(a)$ be the expected sum, over a team's season, of the WP stakes
of the calls it flips, when the team follows $a$ on a call stream drawn like the 2026 games, its opponents play as
recorded, and the player's information is the signal, the count, the inning, the score, the challenges held and the
stake. The benchmark is not known; it is learned from data, and we index the target by the learning. Let $\hat a_{-k}$
be the benchmark learned from a share $(K-1)/K$ of a season's games (engine, decision model, beliefs and DP), and let $V_k(\hat a_{-k})$ be its value on the remaining games. The \emph{accounting index} is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:index}
\theta_K=\frac{1}{30}\,\E\Bigl[\,\sum_{k=1}^K\bigl\{V_k(\hat a_{-k})-V_{{\rm obs},k}\bigr\}\Bigr],
\end{equation}
wins per team-season under the primary intervention, with $V_{{\rm obs},k}$ the realised value of the teams'
challenges on the games of fold $k$ and the expectation over seasons of games like 2026, the benchmark's value being computed in the decision model's
simulated worlds (Section~\ref{sec:honest}). It is the gap to a benchmark a
team could learn from most of a season, not to an oracle that knows the decision model, the beliefs and the stakes
exactly; the oracle gap is larger by the cost of learning, which the coverage study of Section~\ref{sec:eval} puts at
\CPMainLearn{} for the comparator and \CPPrimLearn{} for the primary model. Since $g$ is a
jump in win probability, $\theta_K$ is in expected wins under the accounting that sums the jumps along the recorded
game; we check that accounting, within the model, by re-simulating games (Section~\ref{sec:eval}). It is an accounting
index for one team that deviates alone, not an identified causal effect on real games; wins are zero-sum, so such gains would not add up across teams. A second reading compares the benchmark with the
teams' fitted rule simulated in the same worlds rather than with their realised value (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).

\section{The stakes and their validation}\label{sec:stakes}

Every value in this paper is a sum of ball-versus-strike contrasts $g$, so we validate the contrasts, not the levels
of win probability (Section~\Sref{sec:stakesval}).

\emph{Statcast's own model.} Statcast's win expectancy is an exact function of the pre-pitch state (no spread within
any of its observed cells), so its ball-versus-strike contrast can be read off its own table for \SVSharePct\% of the
called pitches. It correlates with ours at \SVCorr{} (\SVCorrCh{} on challenged pitches); the mean of Statcast's
contrast is \SVRatioPct\% of ours, and \SVRatioChPct\% on the challenged pitches; the two agree in mean within every decile of leverage, though within a decile they rank pitches differently
(correlations \SVDecCorrLo--\SVDecCorrHi; Table~\Sref{tab:svcells}).

\emph{Held-out outcomes.} Within 2\,in of the zone edge the call is decided by fractions of an inch of location; we
\emph{assume} that given the state, ball versus strike is then close to as good as random (a local-randomisation assumption; pre-pitch covariates are balanced between balls and strikes except the strike
count, standardised difference \EdgeStrikesSD, and we have not checked pitcher, umpire or pitch characteristics; the
indicator is the call that stood, after any challenge). If the engine's contrast is right, the batting
team's win indicator $Y$ satisfies $\E(Y\mid x,\text{ball})-\E(Y\mid x,\text{strike})=g(x)$. Fitting the engine on a
random half of the games and regressing $Y-\mathrm{WP}_{\rm strike}(x)$ on $\mathbf 1\{\text{ball}\}\,g(x)$ in the
other half (with $\mathrm{WP}_{\rm strike}$ as a control and game-clustered errors), the slope is \SVSlope{}
\SVSlopeCI{} (\SVN{} pitches; mean over five splits), \SVSlopeHigh{} \SVSlopeHighCI{} in the top leverage tercile,
where the gains are, \SVSlopeSC{} for Statcast's contrast, and \EdgeOrigSlope{} \EdgeOrigSlopeCI{} with the umpire's
original call. A slope of one is what a correct contrast predicts; the intervals are wide, so this is a consistency check
that cannot detect an error of about a quarter in the contrasts, not a validation to that precision. A common rescaling of $g$ rescales the benchmark's continuation values and the gap by the same factor and leaves
its decisions unchanged; a fixed constant $\delta$ would have to be rescaled too.

\emph{A second yardstick.} Scoring every flip at Statcast's independent contrast instead, with decisions on our
full-season engine, gives \SVLostOursSC{} against \SVLostOursSame{} on the same pitches (comparator, in-sample); re-running the
comparator's in-sample analysis (refit, not cross-fitted) on Statcast's stakes, on the shortened stream of the \SVSharePct\%
of called pitches that have a Statcast contrast, gives \SVLostSC{} and the same ranking of every rule.

\section{Out-of-fold evaluation: the full policy cross-fit}\label{sec:honest}

Every learned object is a source of optimism when the benchmark is scored on the games it was learned from: a policy
that selects pitches on estimated stakes picks those whose stake is overestimated; beliefs and continuation values tuned
to the evaluation games fit their noise; and a decision model fitted to them shapes the worlds the benchmark is scored
in. Cross-fitting the whole policy removes the first two and the third's influence on the benchmark's decisions; it does
not remove the decision model's error from the simulated worlds in which those decisions are scored, which is why the
model range and the coverage under misspecification are reported beside the interval. The games are split at random into $K=\GapK$ folds (inside a
bootstrap resample, over the original games, so that copies of a game share a fold). For fold $k$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Learned on the other folds}: the WP engine and the stakes every decision uses, for the held-out pitches
too; the DP's run laws; the decision model, by maximum likelihood on the training opportunities with their training
stakes, and for the primary model the players' posterior effects; the stake terciles of the belief cells and the belief
maps, by isotonic regression of simulated truths on simulated signals of the training pitches; the DP's continuation
values; and the one-line rule's best constant.
\item \emph{Applied, unchanged, to the held-out games}: truths and signals are drawn from the training fit, the
player's belief is the training map of the pitch's cell applied to its signal, and the frozen DP and rules decide along
the recorded stream, holding the challenges they would hold.
\item \emph{Scored on the held-out games alone}: every successful flip, of the benchmark, of each rule and of the
teams' observed challenges, is valued at the signed contrast of an engine fitted to fold $k$ alone (signed, since
clipping a noisy contrast at zero biases it up).
\end{enumerate}
The $K$ folds cover the season once, so the sum over folds estimates~\eqref{eq:index}; $\hat\theta_K$ averages $S=\GapS$
random splits with $R=\GapR$ simulated worlds in each.

\begin{remark}[Out-of-fold scoring]\label{lem:honest}
No decision uses a quantity estimated from fold $k$'s plate-appearance outcomes: the decision stakes, the decision
model, the beliefs and the DP are fitted on the other folds. A policy therefore cannot select pitches on the scoring
engine's estimation error, which is where in-sample scoring is optimistic: for a Gaussian estimate with SD $s$ and a
cutoff $c$, selecting on the estimate and scoring at it overstates the value by $s\,\phi\{(c-g)/s\}$, and scoring at an
independent estimate removes the excess (Section~\Sref{sec:kernel}). This is not a full independence argument. The
decisions still depend on fold $k$ through the states its games visit, and those states are part of the transitions
the fold engine is fitted on, so conditioning on them would nearly fix the scoring contrast; and the observed
challenges, real decisions in the held-out games, are outside the argument altogether. We check the residual
dependence numerically instead: the fold engine's contrast is unbiased against the full-season engine's on the same
pitches, with a ratio of sums of \ScoreRatioAll{} over all called pitches, \ScoreRatioCh{} over the challenged ones and
\ScoreDecLo--\ScoreDecHi{} within deciles of the stake (\nolinkurl{src/scoring_bias.py}).
\end{remark}
\noindent One input is not split: the noise quintiles that define the primary model's belief cells are cut over all
players' fitted noise, a covariate rather than an outcome; cutting them on the training rows only gives
\GapTrainQFive. The same split makes the benchmark's beliefs and DP out-of-sample objects, so~\eqref{eq:index} is the
value of a benchmark a team could learn, which is what an assessment of the teams' play should compare them with.

\emph{What each layer costs.} Table~\ref{tab:recon} collects every estimator and reading of the gap. For the
comparator, scoring in-sample at the full-season stakes gives \WinsLostFull; cross-fitting only the stakes, with the
decision model, beliefs and DP fitted on all games, gives \WinsLost; the full policy cross-fit gives \GapMain. The
selection on estimated stakes is most of the optimism; learning the rest out of sample costs a few hundredths.

\section{Policy values}\label{sec:eval}

\paragraph{Design} Every policy is evaluated on the same simulated worlds: every real 2026 called pitch keeps its known
truth, the ruling is drawn with the mislabel rate, the signal is drawn from the fitted model, the belief is computed
from it, and the policy decides along the real call stream at every opportunity (\NEvalOpp{} pitches), holding the
challenges it would hold. For the primary model each world also draws the players' effects jointly from their
posteriors. All values are full policy cross-fits (Section~\ref{sec:honest}).

\paragraph{Inference} The interval comes from a bootstrap over games \citep{EfronTibshirani1993} that repeats the whole
procedure in every replicate ($B=\GapB$ for each model): the games are
resampled with their Statcast records, a fresh split into folds is drawn over the original games, and within each fold
the engine, the run laws, the decision model --- for the primary model all its hyperparameters, refitted from a warm
start, and the players' posteriors --- the beliefs, the DP and the rules are learned again and scored on the held-out
games ($R=8$ worlds; a replicate of the primary model takes about \GapBootMinutes{} minutes on one worker). Resampling games targets
a population of games exchangeable with the 2026 games (rosters and schedule as in 2026). We report the normal interval, the point $\pm1.96$ bootstrap SDs: \GapCI{} for the primary model and \GapMainCI{} for
the comparator (percentile \GapPct{} and \GapMainPct, basic \GapBasic{} and \GapMainBasic; bootstrap means \GapBootMean{}
and \GapMainBootMean). The bootstrap mean differs from
the point, upwards for the primary model and downwards for the comparator, because a resample repeats about a third of
its games: each training fold then learns the engine, the beliefs and the DP from fewer distinct games, and the
players' posteriors from duplicated data. The bootstrap's spread is right while its location is not: in the coverage study the bootstrap SD matches the
spread of the point across simulated seasons (\CPPrimBootSD{} against \CPPrimPointSD{} for the primary model), and the
intervals that use the bootstrap's location cover less. Hence the normal interval. The choice was made after the coverage study; with \CPPrimS{} seasons the coverage rates
carry a Monte Carlo error of several percentage points, so the study ranks the intervals but does not calibrate them
finely. A replicate also uses one random split and $R=8$ worlds where the point averages $S=\GapS$ splits of
$R=\GapR$; that algorithmic noise is \GapAlgPct\% of the primary bootstrap variance (without it the SD would be
\GapBootSDAlg{} rather than \GapBootSD), so the intervals are slightly conservative on this account. The two models use different convergence criteria: the primary model's Newton refinement stops at a maximum
gradient below $10^{-2}$, which all \GapB{} replicates reached; the comparator's trust-region fit stops at $10^{-4}$
within 60 iterations, which \GapMainBFail{} replicates missed in some fold and which are kept
(Section~\Sref{sec:bootstrap}). The primary model's likelihood is integrated with $11\times11$ nodes; at its fitted parameters $31\times31$ nodes change the negative log-likelihood by \QuadDiff{} points
(\nolinkurl{src/quadrature_check.py}), and we have not refitted with more nodes. The interval is not a guarantee for a non-smooth functional: the benchmark is an
argmax over thresholds and the beliefs are isotonic fits, the setting in which bootstraps of estimated optimal policy
values can fail \citep{ChakrabortyEtAl2013,LuedtkeVanDerLaan2016}. The index sums thousands of threshold decisions,
which smooths it, but we rely on the coverage study, not on a theorem. The intervals are conditional on the
decision model and the intervention: they carry sampling, learning and engine uncertainty, not the model class or
$(\rho,\ell)$, which Section~\ref{sec:pid} treats as a sensitivity analysis.

\paragraph{Coverage} We check the intervals on simulated seasons that keep the real 2026 game stream, so that no game
repeats as it would in a resampled season, and simulate the challenges along it from a generating model; the analysis
is the whole procedure above. Two targets are computed under each generating model: the cross-fit's own target
$\theta_K$ of~\eqref{eq:index}, the benchmark learned from a simulated season and valued in the generating model's world,
and the oracle gap of a benchmark that knows the generating model. With the primary model generating and analysing
(\CPPrimS{} seasons, $B=\CPPrimB$), the point is off $\theta_K$ by \CPPrimBiasK{} (SE \CPPrimBiasSE), its spread across
seasons (\CPPrimPointSD) matches the mean bootstrap SD (\CPPrimBootSD), and the normal interval covers $\theta_K$ in
\CPPrimKNormal\% of seasons, against \CPPrimKPct\% for the percentile and \CPPrimKBasic\% for the basic interval. The
oracle gap is larger by the cost of learning (\CPPrimLearn) and is covered by the normal interval in \CPPrimNormal\%. With
the comparator analysing its own data (\CPMainS{} seasons, $B=\CPMainB$) the point is off by \CPMainBiasK{} and the normal
interval covers in \CPMainKNormal\%. A model without lapses or player effects, applied to data that have them, shifts
the interval rather than widening it: the comparator analysing data from the lapse model and from the primary model (\CPLapseS{} and \CPRES{} seasons) covers $\theta_K$ in
\CPLapseKNormal\% and \CPREKNormal\% and overstates it by \CPLapseBiasK{} and \CPREBiasK; the primary model analysing data from player effects \emph{without} lapses, where it estimates lapses that are not
there, stays on target: it covers in \CPMisKNormal\% of \CPMisS{} seasons and is off by \CPMisBiasK{}
(Section~\Sref{sec:bootstrap}). The richer model protects against the simpler world; the converse does not hold. The seasons keep the 2026 stream and its
plate-appearance outcomes, so the study checks the behavioural component of the interval, not the game-resampling
component.

\paragraph{Policies} Besides the benchmark we run, in the same cross-fit, the one-line rule with $\delta=\RuleDelta$ (the best constant when everything is fitted and scored on the full
season), with $\delta=0.01$ and with the constant that does best on the training folds, the fixed
0.2-run rule of Baseball Savant \citep{Savant2026abs}, ``challenge iff $p\ge0.2/(0.2+\mathrm{RV})$'' with RV the run
value of the flip, and the teams' fitted behavioural rule $s>\kappa(x)$. Under a lapse model every rule is played by the
benchmark's player, who lapses on the same pitches. All rules use the same calibrated beliefs, which nobody using them
in 2026 had, so this compares the rules' targeting, not their real-world use. We also run the other public rules --- the runs-objective rule of \citet{Namuduri2026runs}, the 48-cell card of
\citet{Arora2026use} and Savant's form with a tuned run cost --- and the teams' own rule re-tuned, in the same cross-fit
with every tuned object learned on the training folds (Table~\ref{tab:rules}; \GapExtS{} splits).

\paragraph{Causal accounting} Summing stakes along the recorded game is accounting: after a flipped call the rest of
the game would have differed. We re-simulate in a pitch-level simulator (events, in-play outcomes and baserunning by
count, outs and bases from 2026 Statcast; criterion and belief recomputed at every simulated state), comparing like
with like. The re-simulation uses the comparator with the full-season stakes, so it compares with the uncorrected
\WinsLostFull{} rather than the cross-fitted index. \emph{Paired}: the benchmark and the teams' simulated behavioural rule are played along the stream in the
same world, and from the first pitch at which their flips differ both are re-simulated to the end of the game, each
with its own inventory; the benchmark's gain is \PairRB{} \PairRBCI{} wins per team-season valued by later stakes and
\PairWin{} counting simulated wins, against \PairPath{} by the path sum on the same worlds. \emph{From the recorded
game}: branching from the first pitch at which the benchmark and the recorded challenges differ, and continuing the
team with its fitted rule, gives \FullRB{} against \FullPathObs{} for the path sum against the observed challenges.
The win count sits up to about \WinMinusRBAbs{} below the stake-valued estimate; part of that is the simulator's flip swing,
\SimGShareRunGap\% short of $g$, and the rest we cannot attribute (Section~\Sref{sec:causalgap}). The check is internal
to the model: the engine and the simulator share their structure.

\section{What the data cannot decide}\label{sec:pid}

The index is defined for the primary intervention, which changes only the decision rule. Table~\ref{tab:pid} reports
it, with the same full policy cross-fit, over the perceptual share $\rho$ of the decision noise and the removable share
$\ell$ of the lapses, for the primary model and the comparator (the grid for the other models, cross-fitting the stakes
only, is in Section~\Sref{sec:pidfull}). The grid is a structural sensitivity analysis within the Gaussian decision
model, not a sharp identified set \citep{Manski2003}: we do not claim that the data bound $(\rho,\ell)$, and we do not
attach inference to the grid's corners. Moving away from the primary intervention raises the index, and steeply.
Removing all lapses raises it from \Gap{} to \GapLamOne{} under the primary model; removing half the variance of the decision noise ($\rho=0.5$, about 29\% of its SD) raises it to \GapRhoHalf{} (\GapMainRhoHalf{} under the comparator), and the largest value on the grid,
which stops at $\rho=0.25$, is \GapGridHi. The value of better information is large because the benchmark exploits
sharper beliefs on every pitch. Within each model the primary intervention gives the smallest value on the grid, as Blackwell's ordering predicts
for a Bayes-optimal decision maker \citep{Blackwell1953}; for the estimated benchmark this is a regularity of the table,
not a theorem. The lapse corner also matters: the far misses that are not challenged are partly a
choice, since their challenge rate rises with the stake and falls with one challenge left (Section~\Sref{sec:perception}),
so some of the lapses may be removable. This orders benchmark values within the pooled
model; it is not a lower bound on what a team loses in real games. How much more a team could gain by changing how its players see or attend, the data cannot settle; only an intervention or an independent measurement of perception could.

\section{The mechanism: targeting, not frequency}\label{sec:mech}

The benchmark challenges \GapNMorePct\% more often than teams, but frequency is not where most of the gap is. Under the primary model the one-line rule challenges \GapRuleN{} times per team-game against the teams' \GapNObs{} and
recovers \GapRuleGapPct\% of the gap; at exactly the teams' rate (a mixture of the two grid constants that bracket it)
it recovers \GapFMGapPct\% \GapFMGapCI. The teams' own rule class, which needs no calibrated belief, says the same. In the
full policy cross-fit we re-tune the criterion $s>\kappa(x)$ of each side on the training folds and apply it, frozen, to
the held-out games: shifting only its intercept recovers \GapRetuneIntGapPct\% of the gap, changing only its slope on
$\log g$ \GapRetuneSlopeGapPct\%, and both \GapRetuneBothGapPct\% (comparator: \GapMainRetuneIntGapPct\%,
\GapMainRetuneSlopeGapPct\% and \GapMainRetuneBothGapPct\%). A team that teaches its players to demand less evidence when
more is at stake, without changing how often they challenge on average, recovers most of what the benchmark gains. These are comparisons of interventions within a rule class, not an identified decomposition: changing a criterion's
slope also changes its frequency, timing and inventory. The threshold slopes below are for the comparator.

The threshold slopes say the same. Mapping the fitted criterion to the confidence threshold $p^\ast$ it implies and
regressing $\log\{(1-p^\ast)/p^\ast\}$ on $\log g$ with the criterion's state controls, teams' slope is \SlopeTeamBatter{}
\SlopeTeamBatterCI{} for batters and \SlopeTeamCatcher{} \SlopeTeamCatcherCI{} for catchers; the benchmark's own
thresholds in the same regression on the same pitches give \SlopeOptBatter{} \SlopeOptBatterCI{} and
\SlopeOptCatcher{} \SlopeOptCatcherCI{} (normal intervals from the bootstrap SD); with the twelve exact count cells as controls, \SlopeTeamBatterFE{} and
\SlopeTeamCatcherFE{} against \SlopeOptBatterFE{} and \SlopeOptCatcherFE. On calls wrong by less than an inch, batters
challenge \MFRateLowBatter\% in the lowest stake quintile and \MFRateHighBatter\% in the highest. The stake is the
engine's $g$; players who respond to a cruder perceived stake may respond to it more strongly, so this concerns the
engine's stake, not what players attend to. The game-state terms of Section~\ref{sec:estimates} point the same way: the
criterion falls with two outs (\StateTwoOutsBatter\,in for batters, \StateTwoOutsCatcher\,in for catchers) and with a
runner in scoring position, so players do respond to leverage that the engine's $g$ also carries, and part of the low
slope on $\log g$ is that response routed through the state terms.

Figure~\ref{fig:threshold} shows how the benchmark's threshold moves. Inventory moves it at every score: in the fifth
inning, holding one challenge rather than two raises it by \GapOneTwo{} \GapOneTwoCI{} percentage points in a tied game.
Time moves it mainly through the score: averaged over the score states games visit, the threshold with one challenge
left falls by \GapEarlyLateOneOcc{} \GapEarlyLateOneOccCI{} points from the first to the eighth inning, but in a game that
stays tied by only \GapEarlyLateOne{} \GapEarlyLateOneCI, because a late tie keeps every remaining call valuable.

\section{Robustness}\label{sec:robust}

\emph{Over the season.} Fitted to March--June and predicting July--September (Table~\ref{tab:oos}), batters' decision
noise is stable (\OOSTrainSrBatter{} and \OOSTestSrBatter\,in) but the fielding side's falls from \OOSTrainSrCatcher{}
to \OOSTestSrCatcher\,in, and catchers challenged \OOSCatcherExcess\% more than the first-half fit predicts. The
time-varying model of Table~\ref{tab:variants} absorbs this drift and gives \GapTime{} under the primary intervention;
by calendar month the comparator's gap ranges from \MonthLostLo{} to \MonthLostHi.

\emph{Game state in the criterion.} With the state terms the index is \GapREState{} under the primary model and
\GapState{} under the comparator, against \Gap{} and \GapMain{} without them.

\emph{The estimator's own choices.} How the benchmark's beliefs are learned matters. With four times as many simulated
draws for the belief maps the primary index is \GapNauxEight{} instead of \Gap; with the beliefs calibrated by the exact
count rather than three count classes it is \GapCalFineNauxEight{} (\GapCalFine{} with the usual draws; comparator
\GapMainCalFine), and few held-out pitches fall in cells unseen in training, so this is not a fallback effect. The
calibration cells are a modelling choice about what a player could learn, and the index moves by about 0.1 with it; with $K=2$ and $K=10$ folds the comparator gives \GapMainKTwo{} and
\GapMainKTen{} against \GapMain{} at $K=5$, the cost of learning from a smaller share of the season.

\emph{Recovery.} Simulating the season from the primary model and refitting it, the lapse rates are recovered with bias \RecPLapseBatter{} and \RecPLapseCatcher{} percentage points (SD across
replicates \RecPLapseSDBatter{} and \RecPLapseSDCatcher) and $\sigma_r$ with \RecPSrBatter\% and \RecPSrCatcher\% over
\RecPN{} replicates: under the model the lapse is identified, by the shape, though not precisely. The player-effect SDs
come back smaller (\RecPSdKBatter\% for the batters' criterion), because the simulated players sit at their shrunken
posterior means.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What a team could do} Within the model, challenge when $p\ge\RuleDelta/(g+\RuleDelta)$ with $g$ in win
probability; this is a candidate for prospective testing, not an established prescription. The
rule needs the stake in WP, which a team can tabulate before the game by count, inning and score, and an honest
confidence. The second is the hard part: the benchmark gives every player a calibrated belief, which nobody has been
shown to hold. A practical version is a criterion that falls with the stake, which is what the re-tuned slope of
Section~\ref{sec:mech} does with the information teams have: for a batter under the comparator the one-line rule's
signal threshold falls from about \SRuleLowBatter\,in in the lowest stake quintile to \SRuleHighBatter\,in in the
highest, while the teams' criterion moves only from \KappaLowBatter{} to \KappaHighBatter\,in
(Table~\Sref{tab:mechanism}). Players cannot be helped by the dugout in the challenge window, so
this is a card to learn before the game, not a calculation to make during it. A fixed cost of 0.2 runs, the break-even published on Baseball Savant, performs worst of the rules we evaluate when
it is given the model's beliefs; the same form with a tuned cost does well.

\paragraph{Limitations} (i) The engine is league-average and its count transitions are empirical continuations, not
interventions on the count; its contrasts agree with Statcast's and with held-out outcomes near the edge, which tests
their size on average and in the high-leverage tercile, not every cell. (ii) The counterfactual keeps the real call
stream and the opponents' play; the re-simulation shares the engine's structure. (iii) The benchmark is optimal for a
pooled opportunity model, and we have no bound on what a richer state could add. (iv) The fielding side pools catchers with \NChPitcher{} pitcher challenges as one decision. The lapse rate is identified by the probit's shape, and its interpretation (attention or choice) is open; the grid over $\ell$ is the answer we can give.
The benchmark's beliefs are learned in cells of the player's information, and finer cells move the index by about
0.1. (v) The interval is conditional on the decision model and the
intervention; the model range and the $(\rho,\ell)$ grid are reported beside it, not folded into it. The held-out worlds
are simulated from the training fit, so the cross-fit protects the benchmark's decisions, not the world they are scored
in, against the decision model's errors. (vi) Teams'
criteria shifted during the season, so the 2026 gap may shrink in 2027, a testable prediction. (vii) The benchmark
maximises the probability of winning the game; a player weighing their own plate appearance has a different objective.

\paragraph{Cricket} Cricket's Decision Review System keeps a review that ends in ``umpire's call'', the same
retention structure \citep{Shivakumar2018}. An exploratory application to Cricsheet data \citep{Cricsheet}
(Section~\Sref{sec:drs}) shows why the known truth matters: without it the decision noise is weakly identified, and the
losses, small at the point estimates, are not robust across the noise's own profile interval.

\paragraph{Prior work} The retention DP, the size of the gap and role-specific noise are in public, non-peer-reviewed
work that predates this paper \citep{Arora2026use,Namuduri2026runs,Kirby2026economy,Hires2026sdt,Tango2026naive};
\citet{Hires2026sdt} also fits a signal-detection model to all called pitches classified by the zone rule, without a
state-dependent criterion or policy evaluation. Descriptive analyses include \citet{LeeEtAl2026abs} and
\citet{Baumann2026money}. The umpire's zone has its own statistical literature
\citep{ZimmermanEtAl2019,DeshpandeWyner2017,Mills2017}. The estimand is a sum of win-probability increments, as in
openWAR and nflWAR \citep{BaumerEtAl2015,YurkoEtAl2019}; the comparison of observed with optimal decisions in sport goes
back to \citet{Romer2006}, with \citet{SandholtzEtAl2024} the closest template. Off-policy evaluation
\citep{ThomasBrunskill2016,KallusUehara2020} needs the logging policy's action probabilities given its information,
which here depends on a signal we never observe, so policies are valued through the fitted signal model; inference for estimated optimal policies and treatment rules
\citep{HiranoPorter2009,LaberEtAl2014,LuedtkeVanDerLaan2016,AndrewsEtAl2024} motivates the coverage study, and
\citet{YamLopez2019} value forgone football decisions with a similar win-probability accounting. Criterion noise and
its confounding with perceptual noise are classical in signal detection
\citep{GreenSwets1966,BenjaminEtAl2009,MuellerWeidemann2008}; what we add is to make that non-identification an
explicit dimension of the policy value; the known ABS truth pins down the response curve within the assumed
signal model, not the full set of information structures compatible with the decisions.

\section{Data and code availability}\label{sec:repro}

\emph{Data.} All data are public: pitch tracking from MLB's Statcast (Baseball Savant), challenge events from the MLB
Stats API live game feeds, and ball-by-ball cricket data from Cricsheet. They are not redistributed, under the
providers' terms; the scripts \nolinkurl{src/fetch_*.py} and \nolinkurl{src/drs/fetch.py} re-download them, and
\nolinkurl{results/manifest.json} records SHA-256 checksums of the raw inputs used here, since public feeds can be revised.

\url{https://github.com/HeirOfIsildur/abs-challenge-policy}, release \nolinkurl{v1.0-aoas-submission}, and in the code
supplement. \nolinkurl{rerun.sh} rebuilds the MLB results in dependency order from the fetched data (the full policy
cross-fit is \nolinkurl{src/policy_crossfit.py} and its coverage study \nolinkurl{src/coverage_pcf.py}; the bootstraps of
the primary model need a cluster, about \GapBootHours{} worker-hours; the cricket pipeline is in \nolinkurl{src/drs/}).
Every number, table and figure in the paper and its supplement is written by \nolinkurl{paper/make_numbers.py},
\nolinkurl{paper/supplement_numbers.py} and \nolinkurl{paper/make_figures.py} from \nolinkurl{results/}. The exact algebra
(Proposition~\ref{prop:ident}, Theorem~\ref{thm:mono}, the intervention identities) is checked in
Mathematica~\ProofsVersion{} by the scripts in \nolinkurl{wolfram/}; these verify the algebra, not the assumptions under
which a result applies to the data (Section~\Sref{sec:kernel}).
